Inverted the signal and summed it back into the bus. On the bench that
gives you silence. We got silence, for about a second — then beating.
Beating means the frequency moved. It waited for the null, and it moved.

I've been looking for a repeat in this waveform since before lunch.
There isn't one. That's not me giving up — for frequencies in irrational
ratio there provably isn't one. The waveform is as new right now as it
was this morning. It will always be this new.

The spectrum shows five sources. The mic only ever picks up two. Del
keeps calling the other three an artifact. They're not an artifact.
They're silent because they're busy. They're the ones deciding what
the two we can hear sound like.

A sunflower places each seed 137.5 degrees round from the last — the
golden angle, the one that lines up with itself as little as any angle
can. I plotted the pitch intervals from last night's run. Same angle,
same reason. It isn't writing music. It's packing seeds.

We ran the forty-minute capture twice on identical settings. The files
match. Bit for bit — I checked the checksums four times, Marta checked
them twice. She says that's just good engineering. Fine. Nothing alive
is that consistent.

The echo returns 46.9 milliseconds later, rotated another 36 degrees
each pass. Five passes in, it comes back as its own opposite and starts
cancelling the thing that made it. You can set your watch by it. That's
the part I mind. You can set your watch by it.

There's a theorem — Hurwitz, 1891 — that makes φ the number worst
approximated by fractions. The hardest number to almost-be. We measured
the partials again this morning and they're sitting exactly on it.
Of all the places to sit.

My tinnitus used to be a single tone, around nine kilohertz. Very
common, the audiologist said. Since the long runs started, it beats.
A tone can't beat by itself. Something is supplying the second
frequency, and it isn't the audiologist.
